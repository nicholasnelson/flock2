# Review of progress — 2026-10-24

A read-through of the code, docs and recorded results at `12abf7d` by the cloud session that
wrote the original plan. No tests or runs were executed; everything below is from reading. Where
I quote a number it is from the committed logs in `m1-results/` or the READMEs. Items are ordered
by how much I think they matter.

## Summary

The work since the handover is substantial and, more importantly, honest: nine M0 grids with
every cheat found in the galleries written up, a negative result on the shared-encoder hypothesis
stated plainly, and an M1 pipeline (meadow, first-person view, world model, drives, actor-critic,
curiosity) built with a diagnosis for every failed run. The documentation is better than most
research code I have seen. The two things I would change first are both in the actor-critic's
reward handling, and I think together they explain why no run has yet learned to feed itself:

1. **The return normaliser is crushing the drive signal under varied starts** (§1).
2. **Curiosity is normalised so that it never fades** (§2).

After those, the biggest methodological gap is that M1 agents have **no evaluation protocol,
baselines or seeds** (§3), and the biggest process risk is debugging four learned components at
once in 80-minute runs (§4).

## 1. Return normalisation: the varied starts broke the actor (likely; evidence below)

`ac.train_step` divides advantages by `max(1, S)`, S an EMA of the 5th–95th percentile range of
the λ-returns (DreamerV3's recipe). Look at `ac_ret_scale` in the logs:

| run | start levels | `ac_ret_scale` | `ac_critic_loss` | `ac_actor_loss` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| run 3 (pretrained WM) | generated, 0.6–0.9 | ~10 | ~21 | −0.5 |
| birth1 | generated | **8.9** | ~22 | −0.33 |
| birth2 (`--varied`) | uniform [0.2, 1.0] | **144** | 62–97 | ±0.01 |

With `--varied`, sheep in one batch range from near-dead to full; with reward −D, γ = 0.997 and
a 32-step horizon bootstrapped by the critic, their returns range over roughly [−600, 0], so the
percentile spread S is ~144. An action's real effect over the horizon is of order 0.1–0.5 in
return; divided by 144 it becomes ~0.002, far below the curiosity term (≈ 0.1 per step → ≈ 3 over
the horizon) and comparable to the entropy bonus. The README's own conclusion for birth2 — "a
curiosity bonus of ~0.1 per step outweighed the real reward many times over" — is this
normalisation, not (only) the drives moving slowly. The varied starts were right for the world
model and wrong for this normaliser. DreamerV3's return scaling is designed for tasks whose
*reward* scale is unknown; here the spread it measures is between-sheep differences in state
value, which carry no information about which action is better.

Suggestions, in order of how little they change:
- Normalise **advantages**, not returns: S = EMA of the 5th–95th range of `(ret − value)`. The
  between-state spread disappears from S and the within-state action effect does not.
- Or fix S to a constant per reward design (the expected return range of one sheep's actions).
- Report `ac_ret_scale` and the mean |advantage| in `m1_agent_report.py` so this is visible.

The drive-reduction reward (the Keramati & Gutkin form the plan started with) would also have
had a small return spread, because the telescoping sum removes most of the level dependence. It
was dropped after agent run 1, whose collapse was later traced to the pretraining bug, not to the
reward form. It may be worth keeping both forms available and testing them with the normaliser
fixed; the README's argument that −D is "far easier to predict" for the reward head stands and is
independent of this.

## 2. Curiosity normalised to a fixed mean cannot fade

`intrinsic = curiosity × disagreement / EMA(disagreement)`. The test even asserts it: "curiosity
adds a reward of about its weight". So however much the model learns, the ensemble's remaining
disagreement — however tiny — is rescaled to pay ≈ `curiosity` per step on average, and the
actor is permanently pushed toward its currently most uncertain action with a fixed budget.
Lowering the weight (birth3: 0.1 → 0.02) changes the constant, not the shape. Plan2Explore scales
disagreement by a fixed constant, so curiosity *does* fall as the model learns; that fall is the
whole point of a curiosity drive in a long-running installation.

Suggestions:
- Normalise by a **frozen** scale: the mean disagreement measured at the end of infancy (or a
  running *maximum*), so that as disagreement shrinks the reward shrinks with it.
- Or route curiosity through the interest drive as the survey recommended for exactly this
  reason (an additive bonus outside the drives shifts the effective set-point — Keramati &
  Gutkin's palatability pathology, which the README acknowledges as a risk).
- Learning progress (the owner's proposal, already noted as the later option) solves both this
  and the noisy-TV worry; the delayed-copy version is cheap to add.

Related: the probe's statement that "every action's real reward over 8 steps lies within ±0.01"
is about *healthy scripted sheep* (mean hydration 0.50; probe populations are the scripted flock
warmed up). For a sheep at a deficit of 0.5, eight bites change D by ~0.08 under m = 2 — not
small. So the comparison that justified cutting curiosity to 0.02 is drawn on the wrong
population. The probe should report per-deficit bands (e.g. levels < 0.5) alongside the healthy
mean, which the agent's own replay could supply.

## 3. M1 agents have no evaluation protocol, baselines or seeds

Every M1 agent judgement is read from training-time logs of a single seed. M0 found that one
run in six fails to start communicating; the same seed fragility should be assumed here. The
plan's M1 success criteria are also not yet measurable as written ("no sheep stands still > X%"
with X undefined; "dawn/dusk rhythm" without a statistic).

Suggestions:
- An `m1_eval.py`: freeze the policy, run N fresh meadows for one full day from dawn, and
  report: mean and 10th-percentile levels at dusk and dawn; fraction of sheep with any level at
  0; graze-with-grass and drink-at-water rates; the day/night action mix; distance to cover at
  night. The same script for three baselines: **uniform random**, **the scripted flock** (the
  current upper bound), and a **degenerate "rest always"** policy. Put the baseline rows in the
  README table so "beats a random flock" is a number.
- Two seeds minimum per configuration before a conclusion; three before a design change.
- Define the M1 behavioural criteria numerically now (e.g. ≥ 60 % of night steps resting within
  3 tiles of cover; grazing concentrated in two daytime peaks by a circular-statistics test).

## 4. Four learned components are being debugged at once

Each birth run trains a world model from scratch, a reward head, an actor-critic in imagination
and a curiosity ensemble, for 80 minutes, and the diagnosis of what went wrong is made from the
end state. Three changes per run (birth2, birth3) are acknowledged in the README. This is the
expensive way to find bugs like §1 and §2.

A sanity ladder that isolates components, each a short run:
1. **Privileged policy on the true simulator.** A small MLP on privileged features (levels,
   nearest water/grass/cover vectors, time of day) trained with the real reward by a model-free
   method (PPO) directly in the sim. This answers "is the meadow + drives + reward learnable at
   all, and what does a competent sheep score?" — the upper bound §3 needs — without a world
   model, in minutes. If this cannot learn to drink at water, nothing downstream will.
2. **Actor-critic in imagination on a *true* model.** Replace the learned world model with the
   simulator itself (step the sim inside the imagination rollout from logged states) to test
   `ac.py`'s learning in isolation. The current tests check λ-returns against a hand
   computation and that a step runs; nothing checks that the actor-critic can learn anything.
3. **Then** the learned world model, with the reward head's 15-step correlation (already
   measured, 0.90) as the gate.
4. **Then** curiosity, as one change against a no-curiosity control.

Also: one variable per run from here on, or a 2×2 on the two cheapest, given that runs are
80 minutes and the GPU is otherwise idle overnight.

## 5. The scripted flock loses its weakest tenth

The README's correction is good: the scripted flock's 10th-percentile sheep hit zero energy by
day 3 and zero hydration by the first night. Two consequences not yet drawn out:
- The environment's harshness is unknown. If a privileged, hand-written policy with hysteresis
  loses 10 % of sheep, the drive rates may simply be too fast for the meadow's geometry (water
  10+ tiles away at walking speed 0.12 tiles/step = 80+ steps, with hydration falling 0.0008/step
  and the night 480 steps long). The §4 privileged learner would settle this; so would a quick
  parameter sweep of the script.
- For the installation, a 10 % background death rate from thirst — before any fox — is a
  population problem M2/M7 will inherit. Worth deciding now whether the meadow should be made
  survivable by a competent policy (my recommendation) or whether starvation is part of the show.

## 6. M0: what the closing result does and does not establish

The closing conclusion is right and well argued: communication emerges without encouragement;
the shared encoder does not make drawings iconic; a resemblance loss only satisfies the judge
it is trained against. Three caveats for how M0 carries forward:

- **The "medium" is now a stack of nine designer interventions** (black 2 px lines, pen-lift +
  length cost, delayed start, hard gate, min one stroke, re-centring, jitter, rotation, clutter,
  reduced resolution). Each closed a cheat, each is documented, but the set was accreted on one
  toy, and the plan's M4 will inherit it wholesale. Before M4, re-run the closing cells with the
  channel pressures removed one at a time to learn which are load-bearing in the meadow's own
  reading conditions (which differ: first-person view, "look down" mode) rather than in the toy's.
- **The human quiz was skipped** on the examiner's prediction. It costs twenty minutes with two
  people and would calibrate the examiner — the project's fixed yardstick for M4 — against the

  the best `line_perc` cell (judge 0.80) so the examiner's scores have a human anchor at both
  ends.
- **The examiner guesses "fox" for 64–91 % of drawings**: report balanced accuracy or per-class
  recall alongside accuracy, or its chance level is misleading.

## 7. Surprise: the KL measures information, not novelty

The step-2 result is honest ("not yet selective"). One reason is structural: KL(post ‖ prior)
per step is how much the view tells the model beyond its prediction — turning is "surprising"
because new panorama arrives, which is information, not novelty. A teleport scores 3× the mean
but only 1.3× the 99th percentile because routine viewing is already information-rich. For the
installation's "startle" signal and for M2's fox, two better candidates:

- the **prior's prediction error on the actual frame**, z-scored against a running baseline for
  the current action (turning has its own baseline);
- the **ensemble disagreement** already built for curiosity, which is zero for familiar
  stochastic events by construction — the survey's recommended remedy for the noisy-TV problem.

And a novelty test that matches M2's need: an object that has never appeared (a fox sprite)
entering a familiar view, rather than a teleport.

## 9. Code notes (small; none urgent)

- `scripts/m1_agent.py` loads `m1_wm_train.py` via `importlib` to reuse `train_step`,
  `load_shards`, `sample`: move those into `flock/wm_train.py` and import normally.
- The scripted-shard path in `m1_agent.py` is dead (`raise SystemExit` unless `--scripted none`);
  either re-log the shards with six felt channels or remove the branch and the `--wm_online` knob.
- `act_and_step`: `a_prev` starts as int 0, which is one-hot STOP, whereas `observe` uses a zero
  vector for the first step. Harmless but inconsistent; use −1 and `one_hot(…)` gives zeros.
- `fresh()` moves the flock to a random centre after `generate` kept only the *original* centre
  dry; sheep can start in water or inside a hut (`_allowed` is not checked at spawn). A sheep in
  water can walk out, so this is cosmetic — but it also means `--varied` replay contains
  starts no live sheep will see.
- `Replay.add` copies in a Python loop; fine at 32 sequences per iteration, will not be at 1,000.
  `np.take`/slice assignment with modular indices handles the wrap.
- Hard-coded `16` for `N_COLOURS` in `act_and_step`; `wm.ensemble_logits` is an alias of
  `ensemble_predict`; `fpv.object_billboards` appears unused.
- `drives.reward` takes `h_before` and ignores it; drop the parameter or keep both forms behind
  a flag so §1's comparison is one switch.
- Tests: good coverage of mechanics (collisions, drinking at the edge, facing, energy by action,
  taste). Missing: any test that the actor-critic *learns* (a two-action bandit with a known
  optimum would do), and a test that `fresh()` produces legal positions.
- Sliding-window replay: sequences overlap by 48 of 64 steps, so each frame appears in four
  sequences and the effective replay is a quarter of its nominal 8,192 × 64. Not wrong, but the
  "train ratio 32" is really ~8 on unique frames; worth stating.

## 10. Things I checked and found sound

- The reward head's alignment (state t+1 predicts the reward of step t) is consistent between
  `wm.loss_fn` and `ac.imagine`.
- KL balancing, free bits (1 nat on the summed KL, as DreamerV3), unimix and the straight-through
  sample match the paper; the GRU is the layer-normalised DreamerV3 cell.
- The ensemble's per-action output branches (so an untried action keeps disagreeing) is a
  sensible fix to a real problem, and tested.
- The world-model train ratio per iteration (64 updates × 4 × 64 frames against 512 collected)
  is DreamerV3's 32, modulo the overlap note above.
- Interoception entering as an additive embedding and the taste channel as a sixth felt input
  are clean; the "bad taste" is correctly a fixed aversive signal, not a drive.
- The first-person renderer's benchmark and adoption were done the way the plan asked (spike,
  numbers, then the decision), and the information-gap argument for the blind wedge is sound.
- The scripted-flock correction (means vs 10th percentile) is exactly the kind of honesty the
  project needs; see §5 for what follows from it.
